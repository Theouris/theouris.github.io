Structural Division – Where Structures Are Located

  • The CNS consists of brain and spinal cord; the PNS connects them with receptors and effectors through cranial nerves, spinal nerves, peripheral nerves, and ganglia.
  • A ganglion contains peripheral neuron cell bodies; a nerve contains peripheral axons. A CNS nucleus contains cell bodies; a tract contains CNS axons.
  • Localize before naming a disease: determine whether findings implicate cortex, brainstem, spinal cord, root, plexus, peripheral nerve, neuromuscular junction, or muscle.

Functional Organization of the Nervous System

  • Afferent means toward the CNS; efferent means away from the CNS. These describe information flow, not whether a structure is physically central or peripheral.
  • Somatic afferents convey skin, muscle, and joint sensation. Visceral afferents convey internal-organ information.
  • Somatic motor output controls skeletal muscle. Autonomic motor output controls smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands through sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions.
  • Voluntary/involuntary is a useful overview, but somatic reflexes can be involuntary; the effector and pathway define the division.

Lecture 1.1 · Slide 5

Spinal Nerve

  • Ventral/anterior roots carry efferents: somatic motor axons arise from anterior horn cells; autonomic preganglionic axons arise from intermediate gray matter at appropriate levels.
  • Dorsal/posterior roots carry afferents. Primary sensory neuron cell bodies reside in the dorsal root ganglion, outside the cord; most are pseudounipolar and do not synapse in that ganglion.
  • Roots unite into a short mixed spinal nerve that exits the intervertebral foramen, then divides into mixed dorsal and ventral rami.
  • Dorsal rami supply intrinsic back muscles and overlying skin; ventral rami supply the anterolateral trunk and limbs and form major plexuses. A posterior ramus is not a posterior root.
  • A dorsal root lesion preferentially impairs sensation; a ventral root lesion preferentially impairs motor output; a mixed nerve or ramus lesion can affect both.

Autonomic Nervous System

  • Both autonomic divisions regulate visceral effectors, often with opposing actions but sometimes with complementary or exclusive innervation.
  • Sympathetic outflow is thoracolumbar, T1-L2; parasympathetic outflow is craniosacral, through CN III, VII, IX, X and S2-S4.
  • The usual autonomic efferent chain is preganglionic neuron in CNS → autonomic ganglion → postganglionic neuron → effector. Visceral afferents are separate sensory neurons.

Somatic vs Autonomic Motor Pathways

  • The final somatic efferent pathway uses one lower motor neuron from CNS to skeletal muscle; descending upper motor neurons are upstream and are not counted in this comparison.
  • Autonomic ganglia contain the synapse between preganglionic and postganglionic neurons. Sensory ganglia generally do not contain such relay synapses.
  • Somatic neuromuscular transmission uses acetylcholine at nicotinic muscle receptors; autonomic ganglia use acetylcholine at nicotinic neuronal receptors.
  • The adrenal medulla is a modified sympathetic ganglion: preganglionic fibers directly activate chromaffin cells, which release catecholamines into blood.

Sympathetic System: Thoracolumbar Outflow

  • Preganglionic sympathetic cell bodies lie in the intermediolateral column/lateral horn of T1-L2 cord segments.
  • Trace the entry route: lateral horn → ventral root → spinal nerve → white ramus communicans → sympathetic trunk.
  • Inside the trunk, a fiber may synapse at its entry level, ascend or descend then synapse, or pass through toward an abdominal prevertebral ganglion.
  • Ascending fibers allow thoracic outflow to reach cervical sympathetic ganglia and the head; descending fibers allow it to reach lumbar and sacral levels.

White and Gray Rami Communicantes

  • White rami carry myelinated preganglionic sympathetic efferents into the chain and are present at T1-L2 only; they also carry visceral afferents traveling back toward the cord.
  • Gray rami carry unmyelinated postganglionic sympathetic efferents from the chain back to spinal nerves at every spinal level.
  • Those fibers distribute through dorsal and ventral rami to cutaneous vessels, sweat glands, and arrector pili in the trunk and limbs.
  • A cervical spinal nerve has a gray ramus despite lacking a white ramus: its preganglionic input ascended from thoracic levels.

Sympathetic Splanchnic Nerves

  • Thoracic and lumbar abdominopelvic splanchnic nerves primarily carry preganglionic sympathetic fibers through the chain to prevertebral ganglia; accompanying visceral afferents travel in the opposite direction.
  • Sacral splanchnic nerves arise from the sacral sympathetic trunk and primarily carry postganglionic sympathetic fibers to the inferior hypogastric plexus.
  • Pelvic splanchnic nerves arise from S2-S4 and carry preganglionic parasympathetic fibers; pelvic and sacral splanchnics are different nerves.
  • Not every splanchnic nerve is preganglionic sympathetic: cardiopulmonary splanchnics are postganglionic sympathetic, while pelvic splanchnics are parasympathetic.

Sympathetic Innervation of Abdominal Viscera

  • Abdominal sympathetic route: cord → ventral root → spinal nerve → white ramus → sympathetic chain without synapse → thoracic/lumbar splanchnic nerve → prevertebral ganglion → periarterial plexus → organ.
  • Celiac ganglia/plexus primarily serve foregut; superior mesenteric primarily serves midgut; inferior mesenteric primarily serves hindgut.
  • The gut receives postganglionic fibers along arterial branches rather than through gray rami; gray rami primarily return sympathetic fibers to spinal nerves for the body wall and limbs.

Parasympathetic System: Craniosacral Outflow

  • Cranial parasympathetic outflow travels in III, VII, IX, and X; sacral outflow arises from S2-S4. Preganglionic fibers are generally long, with ganglia close to or inside the target and short postganglionic fibers.
  • CN III → ciliary ganglion → pupil constriction and accommodation; CN VII → pterygopalatine and submandibular ganglia → lacrimal/nasal and submandibular/sublingual secretion.
  • CN IX → otic ganglion → parotid secretion. CN X supplies thoracic viscera and foregut/midgut through terminal ganglia.
  • Pelvic splanchnics supply hindgut and pelvic viscera; cranial ganglion outputs often hitchhike on trigeminal branches, which do not themselves originate parasympathetic outflow.

Pelvic Autonomics: Hypogastric Plexuses

  • The abdominal aortic plexus continues into the superior hypogastric plexus, then right and left hypogastric nerves, then paired inferior hypogastric plexuses.
  • Inferior hypogastric plexuses receive sympathetic hypogastric and sacral splanchnic fibers plus parasympathetic pelvic splanchnics, with visceral afferents also present.
  • These mixed plexuses distribute to pelvic viscera and contribute routes to hindgut. Injury during pelvic surgery can impair bladder, bowel, and sexual function.
  • Distinguish the mixed inferior hypogastric plexus from the parasympathetic pelvic splanchnic nerves entering it.

Enteric Nervous System

  • The enteric nervous system is an intrinsic gut network capable of organizing local reflexes without direct CNS commands, while being modulated by autonomic inputs.
  • Myenteric/Auerbach plexus lies between circular and longitudinal muscle of muscularis externa and primarily coordinates motility.
  • Submucosal/Meissner plexus lies in the submucosa and primarily regulates secretion, mucosal function, and local blood flow.
  • Hirschsprung disease is congenital absence of enteric ganglion cells in distal bowel: failure of relaxation produces functional obstruction, a narrow distal segment, and proximal dilation.

Autonomic Innervation of the GI Tract

  • Sympathetic supply: thoracic splanchnics for foregut/midgut, primarily lumbar splanchnics for hindgut; synapse in prevertebral ganglia, then follow arteries.
  • Parasympathetic supply: vagus for foregut/midgut, pelvic splanchnics for hindgut; synapse near/within the organ.
  • Sympathetic activity generally reduces gut motility and secretion and promotes sphincter contraction; parasympathetic activity generally promotes digestion through enteric circuits.

Somatic vs Visceral Sensory Pathways

  • Somatic afferents convey touch, pain, temperature, and proprioception from skin, skeletal muscle, and joints; spinal cell bodies lie in DRG and axons enter posterior roots.
  • Visceral afferents convey distension, chemical milieu, and pain from viscera/vessels; cell bodies lie in spinal or cranial sensory ganglia.
  • Many visceral pain fibers accompany sympathetic nerves; many physiologic reflex afferents accompany parasympathetic routes, especially vagus. Their direction remains afferent regardless of the motor nerves they accompany.
  • Pelvic pain below the pelvic pain line often follows pelvic parasympathetic pathways; the sympathetic association is not universal.
  • Referred pain reflects convergence of visceral and somatic afferents onto shared spinal neurons; the brain misattributes visceral activity to a somatic dermatome.

Terms of Orientation

  • In spinal cord and most brainstem: dorsal = posterior, ventral = anterior, rostral = toward head, caudal = toward feet.
  • In the forebrain, the bent neuraxis changes these relationships: dorsal = superior, ventral = inferior, rostral = anterior, caudal = posterior.
  • Sagittal sections separate left/right; coronal sections separate anterior/posterior; axial/horizontal sections separate superior/inferior in the brain.
  • Ipsilateral means same side; contralateral means opposite side. Decussation means crossing of fibers, which determines the side of deficits.

Central Nervous System Overview

  • Cerebrum supports perception, voluntary movement, cognition, language, and behavior; deep diencephalic structures include thalamus and hypothalamus.
  • Brainstem consists of midbrain, pons, and medulla; contains long tracts, many cranial nerve nuclei, and vital autonomic circuitry.
  • Cerebellum coordinates timing, balance, and motor learning. Spinal cord relays ascending and descending pathways and supports segmental reflexes.
  • Related developmental framework: telencephalon → cerebral hemispheres; diencephalon → thalamus/hypothalamus; mesencephalon → midbrain; metencephalon → pons/cerebellum; myelencephalon → medulla. Gray Matter vs. White Matter

  • Gray matter is enriched in neuronal somata, dendrites, synapses, glia, and relatively unmyelinated processes; white matter is enriched in myelinated axons and supporting glia.
  • Brain has superficial cortical gray matter over white matter, plus deep gray nuclei: the outside/inside rule is a simplification, not an absolute.
  • Spinal cord has central butterfly-shaped gray matter with dorsal sensory and ventral motor horns, surrounded by white-matter columns.
  • Gray-matter injury may disrupt local processing; white-matter injury may disconnect pathways. Either can cause weakness or sensory loss; symptoms alone do not uniquely distinguish them.

Neuron

  • Dendrites usually receive input; soma integrates and maintains the cell; axon carries output to terminals, where neurotransmitters are released at synapses.
  • Action potentials usually initiate at the axon initial segment near the hillock, a region enriched in voltage-gated sodium channels.
  • Tracts/nuclei belong to CNS; nerves/ganglia belong to PNS. Basal ganglia is a traditional naming exception for CNS nuclei.
  • Most mature CNS neurons have very limited replacement after injury. Timely restoration of perfusion can preserve viable ischemic tissue; PNS axonal regeneration is more favorable when soma and guidance pathways remain intact.
  • Diagram labels: dendrites, soma/nucleus, axon hillock and initial segment, axon, myelin sheath, nodes of Ranvier, and axon terminals. Myelin covers internodes; exposed nodes regenerate action potentials during saltatory conduction.

High-Yield Glial Cells

  • Astrocytes support the neurovascular unit, buffer extracellular potassium, take up neurotransmitters, and form glial scars; GFAP is a useful marker. Endothelial tight junctions physically form the blood-brain barrier; astrocytic endfeet help maintain it.

Intracranial Hematomas

  • Epidural hematoma: classically middle meningeal arterial injury after pterion trauma; a possible lucid interval followed by deterioration; CT typically shows biconvex/lentiform blood limited by sutures.

Herniation Syndromes

  • A mass or swelling raises intracranial pressure and creates gradients that displace tissue through fixed openings or under folds.
  • Subfalcine: cingulate gyrus moves under falx; ACA compression can infarct medial motor cortex and cause contralateral leg-predominant weakness.
  • Uncal: medial temporal uncus crosses tentorial edge; superficial parasympathetic fibers of ipsilateral CN III are compressed, producing a dilated poorly reactive pupil.
  • Uncal peduncle compression usually causes contralateral weakness; compression of the opposite peduncle against tentorium (Kernohan notch) can produce ipsilateral weakness, a false-localizing sign.
  • Central downward transtentorial herniation compromises diencephalon/midbrain and consciousness. Tonsillar herniation pushes cerebellar tonsils through foramen magnum and threatens medullary respiratory/cardiovascular centers.
  • These patterns can progress to coma, cardiorespiratory failure, and death. Cushing response is hypertension/widened pulse pressure, bradycardia, and irregular respiration.

Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF)

  • CSF is clear and colorless, produced mainly by choroid plexus in lateral, third, and fourth ventricles. The cerebral aqueduct has no choroid plexus.
  • Choroid plexus consists of vascular connective tissue with fenestrated capillaries covered by specialized epithelial cells derived from ependyma; their tight junctions form the blood-CSF barrier.
  • Trace the entire route: lateral ventricles → Monro → third → cerebral aqueduct → fourth → Luschka/Magendie → cisterns/subarachnoid spaces around brain and cord → arachnoid granulations → dural venous sinuses.
  • CSF provides buoyancy, cushioning, and chemical homeostasis. Adult CSF volume is roughly 150 mL; production is about 500 mL/day, so it turns over multiple times daily.
  • BBB tight junctions lie between CNS capillary endothelial cells; blood-CSF barrier tight junctions lie between choroid plexus epithelial cells. Ordinary ventricular ependyma is not the same tight barrier.

Hydrocephalus

  • Hydrocephalus is excess CSF accumulation causing ventricular enlargement, with or without persistently high measured ICP.
  • Mechanisms: obstruction within ventricular pathways, impaired outflow/absorption after CSF leaves the ventricles, or rarely overproduction such as choroid plexus papilloma.
  • Raised-pressure symptoms include headache, nausea/vomiting, papilledema, and cognitive or neurologic decline. Infants can expand the skull, causing increasing head circumference and a bulging fontanelle.
  • Ventriculomegaly is an imaging description; it does not by itself prove active pressure-driven hydrocephalus.

Communicating Hydrocephalus

  • Communicating hydrocephalus retains communication from ventricles to the subarachnoid compartment; impaired resorption is the classic mechanism.
  • SAH and meningitis can scar CSF outflow/arachnoid granulations, causing enlargement of the entire ventricular system.
  • An enlarged fourth ventricle does not alone distinguish communicating hydrocephalus from fourth-outlet obstruction; location and patency of CSF pathways matter.

Non-Communicating (Obstructive) Hydrocephalus

  • Noncommunicating hydrocephalus means obstruction along the ventricular outflow pathway; cavities upstream enlarge while downstream cavities are relatively spared.
  • Monro obstruction → one or both lateral ventricles; aqueduct obstruction → both lateral plus third, with fourth spared; fourth-outlet obstruction → enlargement extending through fourth.
  • Causes include congenital malformations, tumors, cysts, and hemorrhage. Always localize the bottleneck using the pattern rather than labeling every enlarged ventricle the same way.

Aqueductal Stenosis

  • Aqueductal stenosis blocks third-to-fourth flow through the midbrain; both lateral ventricles and third enlarge while the fourth is relatively normal.
  • It can be congenital or acquired from local disease, including compression by a mass. Infants may show macrocephaly; older patients show pressure symptoms and reduced consciousness.
  • A shunt diverts CSF; selected obstructive cases can be treated with endoscopic third ventriculostomy, creating a route from third ventricular floor into basal cisterns.

Chiari II Malformation (Arnold-Chiari Malformation)

  • Chiari II is a crowded/small posterior fossa with downward displacement of hindbrain structures, commonly accompanied by obstructed CSF flow and hydrocephalus.
  • Myelomeningocele is the strong associated spinal defect; feeding difficulty, weak cry, stridor, and other brainstem dysfunction may occur in infancy.

  • The slide's 'tonsillar herniation only' is a study shorthand; Chiari I can have additional CSF-flow and spinal-cord consequences.

Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension (Pseudotumor Cerebri)

  • IIH causes elevated ICP without mass lesion or hydrocephalus; ventricular size is typically normal and CSF composition is normal.
  • Typical vignette: young woman with obesity, headache, transient visual obscurations, papilledema, and pulsatile tinnitus; CN VI palsy can cause horizontal diplopia.
  • Diagnosis requires exclusion of other causes, including cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, with brain imaging and usually venous imaging before LP; adult opening pressure is generally at least 25 cm H2O in the appropriate setting.
  • Weight reduction and acetazolamide reduce pressure; threatened vision requires urgent specialist-directed intervention. Transverse sinus narrowing is associated with IIH.
  • Secondary intracranial hypertension can be associated with tetracyclines or vitamin A derivatives; identify secondary causes before calling disease idiopathic.

Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus (NPH)

  • NPH usually affects older adults and causes gait disturbance, cognitive impairment, and urinary urgency/incontinence. Gait dysfunction often appears first and can be magnetic or shuffling.
  • Imaging shows ventricular enlargement disproportionate to cortical sulcal enlargement. A single LP opening pressure can be normal despite abnormal CSF dynamics over time.
  • Impaired CSF absorption and periventricular circuit dysfunction are classic explanations; compression/stretching near corona radiata helps explain gait and bladder findings.
  • Improvement after removal of CSF during a large-volume tap or drainage trial supports likely response to shunting. Selected patients benefit from a ventriculoperitoneal shunt; the triad alone does not establish the diagnosis.

Hydrocephalus Ex Vacuo

  • Ex vacuo enlargement occurs because brain tissue is lost, leaving compensatory expansion of ventricles and cortical sulci.
  • Examples include Alzheimer disease, remote infarction, and other causes of atrophy; the process is not primarily blocked CSF flow or failed absorption.
  • Pressure is generally normal, and CSF diversion does not address the underlying tissue loss. Compare proportional sulcal enlargement with NPH's disproportionate ventriculomegaly.

Autonomic transmitters, receptors, and clinical localization

  • All autonomic preganglionic neurons release acetylcholine onto nicotinic neuronal (Nn) receptors. Somatic motor neurons release acetylcholine onto nicotinic muscle (Nm) receptors.
  • Parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors. Most sympathetic postganglionic neurons release norepinephrine onto alpha/beta receptors.
  • Exception: sympathetic fibers to eccrine sweat glands release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors. Adrenal chromaffin cells receive preganglionic acetylcholine/Nn input and secrete catecholamines into blood.
  • Alpha-1 activation contracts vascular smooth muscle and iris radial dilator; beta-1 increases cardiac rate/contractility and renin release; beta-2 relaxes bronchial smooth muscle. M2 slows the heart; M3 promotes glandular secretion and contracts detrusor and iris sphincter.
  • Horner syndrome is interruption of the sympathetic pathway to the eye: ipsilateral miosis, mild ptosis, and variable anhidrosis. Contrast the dilated pupil of compressive CN III palsy.
  • Bladder storage: sympathetic hypogastric input relaxes detrusor through beta-3 and contracts outlet through alpha-1; pudendal somatic S2-S4 input contracts external sphincter. Voiding: pelvic parasympathetic S2-S4 input contracts detrusor, with coordinated outlet relaxation.
  • Erection depends strongly on pelvic parasympathetic pathways; emission depends strongly on sympathetic pathways, while expulsion also uses somatic muscles. Pelvic surgery can injure mixed autonomic plexuses.
  • Greater thoracic splanchnic nerve is typically T5-T9 to celiac ganglia; lesser T10-T11 and least T12 supply more caudal prevertebral/aorticorenal pathways. These are useful typical ranges, with anatomical variation.
  • White rami and roots are different: a white ramus joins a mixed spinal nerve to sympathetic chain; a ventral root leaves the cord. Parasympathetic outflow does not enter the sympathetic chain via white rami.

Board-review addition

Neurons, myelin, and lesion localization

  • A reflex arc contains receptor → afferent neuron → spinal integration → efferent neuron → effector. Stretch reflexes have a direct sensory-motor synapse, although associated circuitry includes interneurons.
  • Upper motor neuron injury typically causes weakness, spasticity, hyperreflexia, and an extensor plantar response after the acute phase. Acute severe CNS injury can initially be flaccid.
  • Lower motor neuron injury causes weakness, reduced tone/reflexes, denervation atrophy, and possible fasciculations. Anterior horn, ventral root, and peripheral motor axon lesions can all produce this pattern.
  • Corticospinal fibers mostly cross at caudal medullary pyramids: lesions above crossing cause contralateral limb weakness; spinal tract lesions below crossing cause ipsilateral weakness below the lesion.
  • Dorsal-column vibration/proprioception fibers cross in medulla; spinothalamic pain/temperature fibers cross near spinal entry. This explains different sensory laterality in cord lesions.
  • Myelin raises membrane resistance and lowers capacitance; impulses regenerate at nodes of Ranvier. Oligodendrocyte injury affects CNS pathways; Schwann-cell injury affects peripheral pathways.
  • Optic neuritis with painful monocular visual loss suggests CNS demyelination; ascending weakness and areflexia after infection suggests a peripheral polyradiculoneuropathy such as Guillain-Barré syndrome.
  • Satellite glia support sensory/autonomic ganglion somata. A ganglion is a location, not a guarantee of a synapse: autonomic ganglia contain synapses, whereas DRG generally do not.

Board-review addition

Skull-base and dural-sinus landmarks

  • Cribriform plate transmits CN I; optic canal transmits CN II and ophthalmic artery; superior orbital fissure transmits III, IV, V1, VI and ophthalmic veins.
  • Foramen rotundum transmits V2; foramen ovale transmits V3; foramen spinosum transmits middle meningeal vessels. The internal carotid travels through carotid canal, not through the cartilage-filled foramen lacerum.
  • Internal acoustic meatus transmits VII and VIII; jugular foramen transmits IX, X, XI and venous outflow; hypoglossal canal transmits XII. Foramen magnum transmits the cervicomedullary junction, vertebral arteries, and spinal roots of XI among other structures.
  • Superior sagittal sinus → confluence → transverse → sigmoid sinus → internal jugular vein is a useful superficial venous drainage sequence. Inferior sagittal sinus joins great cerebral vein to form straight sinus.
  • Cavernous sinus contains internal carotid artery and CN VI centrally; III, IV, V1, and V2 run in its lateral wall. Facial/orbital infection can spread through venous connections and cause fever, proptosis, ophthalmoplegia, and facial sensory loss.
  • The facial and emissary venous routes can allow infection to reach intracranial sinuses. Dural sinuses are endothelial-lined venous channels without the muscular walls of ordinary veins.
  • Meningiomas arise from arachnoid-associated cells, often form dural-based masses with a dural tail, and can show whorls and psammoma bodies; the tumor is not simply a neoplasm of dense dural connective tissue.

Board-review addition

Intracranial bleeding and pressure: testable reasoning

  • Aneurysmal SAH can cause rebleeding, delayed cerebral ischemia associated with vasospasm, and communicating hydrocephalus. Nimodipine improves outcomes by reducing delayed ischemic injury; definitive aneurysm treatment uses clipping or endovascular techniques.
  • Noncontrast CT is the initial test for suspected acute SAH. If CT is negative but suspicion remains, further assessment such as LP or vascular imaging depends on timing and clinical circumstances; a negative CT does not always end the evaluation.
  • Xanthochromia reflects hemoglobin breakdown in CSF and supports preceding hemorrhage; it takes time to develop. RBC clearance across collection tubes alone cannot reliably exclude SAH.
  • Monro-Kellie doctrine: within the rigid adult skull, brain, blood, and CSF share a nearly fixed total volume. Initial compensatory displacement has limits; further volume increase can sharply raise ICP.
  • Cerebral perfusion pressure is approximately mean arterial pressure minus ICP. Increasing ICP can reduce perfusion even when systemic blood pressure appears adequate.
  • Uncal compression can affect CN III, cerebral peduncle, and posterior cerebral artery; the latter can produce contralateral homonymous field loss.
  • Papilledema reflects optic disc swelling from raised ICP, often bilateral. A CN VI palsy can be a false-localizing sign of raised pressure because of the nerve's long intracranial course.
  • Do not perform an LP in a patient with suspected dangerous intracranial pressure gradients from a mass or obstructive process before appropriate evaluation; removing lumbar CSF can precipitate herniation.

Board-review addition

Blood-brain barrier, blood-CSF barrier, and ventricular lining

  • BBB barrier-forming structure: nonfenestrated capillary endothelial cells joined by tight junctions. Basement membrane, pericytes, and astrocyte endfeet support the neurovascular unit.
  • Blood-CSF barrier-forming structure: tight junctions between choroid plexus epithelial cells. The underlying choroidal capillaries are fenestrated.
  • Ordinary ventricular ependyma generally permits exchange between CSF and interstitial fluid and is not equivalent to the tight choroid epithelial barrier.
  • Choroid plexus actively secretes CSF rather than simply filtering plasma; carbonic anhydrase participates in secretion, explaining acetazolamide's pressure-lowering effect.
  • Inflammation can increase barrier permeability and CSF protein. Normal CSF has low protein and very few cells compared with blood.
  • Circumventricular structures such as area postrema have specialized permeable capillaries that permit sensing circulating signals; the BBB is not uniformly identical everywhere.
  • Ependymoma can obstruct CSF flow, classically in the fourth ventricular region in children; choroid plexus papilloma may cause hydrocephalus through overproduction and/or obstruction.
  • Tumor links: astrocytic differentiation often shows GFAP; vestibular schwannoma arises from CN VIII Schwann cells; ependymoma can show perivascular pseudorosettes and less commonly true ependymal rosettes. These are histologic associations, not substitutes for modern molecular classification.

Board-review addition

Lumbar puncture and spinal meningeal anatomy

  • Adult spinal cord usually ends near L1-L2, while the dural sac/subarachnoid space extends to about S2. LP is commonly performed at L3-L4 or L4-L5 in the lumbar cistern among cauda equina roots.
  • A midline needle passes through skin, subcutaneous tissue, supraspinous ligament, interspinous ligament, ligamentum flavum, epidural space, dura, and arachnoid into subarachnoid CSF.
  • An epidural anesthetic is delivered outside dura; spinal/intrathecal anesthetic enters subarachnoid CSF. The spinal epidural space contains fat and veins.
  • Measure opening pressure with proper lateral-decubitus positioning and interpret with clinical context; ranges vary with technique and patient factors.
  • Assess for mass-related herniation risk, local infection, and significant bleeding risk before LP. Focal deficits, papilledema, or impaired consciousness can prompt brain imaging first.
  • Low-pressure post-dural-puncture headache classically worsens upright and improves lying flat; persistent cases may be treated with an epidural blood patch.
  • Pia forms denticulate ligaments that help anchor the cord and contributes to filum terminale; arachnoid and dura extend beyond the conus to enclose the lumbar cistern.

Board-review addition

CSF patterns and meningeal inflammation

  • Interpret opening pressure, cell count/differential, protein, glucose relative to serum, microbiology, and timing together. Typical adult CSF has approximately 0-5 WBC/µL, protein about 15-45 mg/dL, and glucose roughly 60% of serum, with laboratory-specific ranges.
  • Typical bacterial meningitis: neutrophilic pleocytosis, high protein, low glucose, often high pressure. Early disease, immunosuppression, and partial treatment can alter the pattern.
  • Typical viral meningitis: lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal glucose, and mildly elevated protein; early viral infection can have neutrophils.
  • TB/fungal meningitis often causes lymphocytic cells, high protein, and low glucose. The pattern overlaps with other diseases and is not organism-specific.
  • Guillain-Barré syndrome classically has albuminocytologic dissociation: increased CSF protein with relatively few cells; findings can be normal early. MS may have CSF-restricted oligoclonal IgG bands.
  • Fever, headache, and neck stiffness suggest meningeal inflammation; altered mental status or focal deficits raise concern for additional CNS involvement. Suspected bacterial meningitis needs prompt treatment; a necessary imaging delay should not delay empiric therapy.
  • Meningitis and SAH can impair CSF resorption and produce communicating hydrocephalus; distinguish this downstream complication from a focal aqueductal blockage.

Board-review addition

Ventricular pattern comparison and congenital associations

  • One enlarged lateral ventricle suggests ipsilateral Monro obstruction; both lateral plus third with normal fourth suggests aqueduct obstruction; dilation through fourth suggests fourth-outlet or downstream outflow disease.
  • Communicating hydrocephalus: no focal intraventricular block, commonly impaired absorption after blood or inflammation. Noncommunicating: a focal ventricular/outlet obstruction. Overproduction is uncommon.
  • IIH: high pressure, usually normal-sized ventricles, normal CSF composition, papilledema/visual symptoms. Exclude mass and venous thrombosis; protect vision.
  • NPH: older adult, early gait impairment plus cognitive and urinary symptoms, ventriculomegaly disproportionate to atrophy, potentially normal spot pressure. CSF-drainage response helps select shunt candidates.
  • Ex vacuo: ventricles and sulci enlarge with tissue loss; it is compensatory ventriculomegaly rather than pressure-driven hydrocephalus.
  • Chiari I: low tonsils, often later headache and possible syrinx. A cervical syrinx can injure crossing spinothalamic fibers, causing bilateral suspended/cape-like pain-temperature loss with relative preservation of vibration.
  • Chiari II: crowded posterior fossa, hindbrain descent, myelomeningocele, and frequent hydrocephalus. Dandy-Walker: enlarged posterior fossa, hypoplastic vermis, and cystically expanded fourth ventricle.
  • A ventriculoperitoneal shunt diverts ventricular CSF to peritoneum. Endoscopic third ventriculostomy bypasses selected third-to-fourth obstructions but still requires useful downstream subarachnoid absorption.
