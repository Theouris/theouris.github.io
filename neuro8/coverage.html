Source qualifications and corrections

  • Clinical patterns are probabilistic. Discharge/matting increases the likelihood of bacterial conjunctivitis but does not reliably separate all cases. Severe pain, photophobia, or reduced acuity needs consideration of keratitis or intraocular disease.
  • Uveitis has major infectious, immune-mediated, and idiopathic categories. Toxoplasma is a protozoan, not a fungus, and is an important cause of posterior retinochoroiditis rather than uniformly rare. CMV retinitis is especially relevant with profound cellular immune suppression.
  • Nontypeable H. influenzae is an important AOM pathogen, but “the leading cause” varies by population, vaccination, and sampling. Hib invasive disease and nontypeable middle-ear disease are distinguished. A red drum or fluid alone does not prove AOM; acute bulging and symptoms matter.
  • Postinfectious ADEM is immune-mediated demyelination, not a synonym for every postinfectious encephalopathy. Myelitis means spinal cord inflammation; peripheral nerve weakness is a different localization. Transverse myelitis can be asymmetric and initially flaccid during spinal shock.
  • The International Encephalitis Consortium definition requires altered mental status for at least 24 hours without another cause, with two minor criteria for possible and at least three for probable classification. Minor criteria are fever, new seizures, focal findings, pleocytosis, compatible MRI, and EEG; headache is not itself a formal minor criterion. Confirmed disease needs additional etiologic/pathologic evidence. These classification rules must not delay urgent treatment.
  • Bacterial/viral CSF cell counts, protein, and glucose ranges overlap. Early viral disease can have neutrophils; Listeria can have mixed/mononuclear cells. Some viral infections lower glucose, and severe immune suppression can blunt cells. Use concurrent serum glucose and organism-directed tests rather than a single closed-range rule.
  • Gram stain/culture can be negative after antibiotics. PCR panels cover selected pathogens only. AFB smear is insensitive; TB NAAT and culture remain important. CSF VDRL is specific but insensitive; treponemal tests are more sensitive but need contextual interpretation. Cryptococcal antigen is targeted to Cryptococcus, not a universal fungal screen.
  • CSF eosinophilia is particularly useful for selected helminthic diseases, not every parasite. Naegleria PAM often has neutrophils and can resemble bacterial meningitis; Toxoplasma is often primarily parenchymal.
  • The enterovirus serotype table retains historical nomenclature, including HAV as “enterovirus 72.” HAV is now classified as Hepatovirus. Exact source serotype lists and specimen-detection timelines are preserved for reference; clinical associations are overlapping rather than exclusive.
  • Nonblanching petechiae can accompany several illnesses, but fever with petechiae/purpura warrants urgent evaluation for invasive disease. A glass test does not identify a virus or exclude meningococcemia.
  • HSV encephalitis often involves temporal regions but is not restricted to exactly one lobe. CSF erythrocytes are neither required nor specific; traumatic taps can also introduce red cells.
  • Rabies incubation varies beyond the illustrative table limits. Neural transport can involve more than the dorsal-root route pictured. Natural antibody timing is variable, so waiting for antibody or symptoms is inappropriate after a concerning exposure. Clinical rabies is nearly always fatal, with rare exceptions. PEP details depend on prior vaccination, immune status, and exposure risk.
  • Fungal disease can involve meninges, vessels, and parenchyma. The table’s plus signs and common/occasional labels are qualitative, not universal incidence estimates. “Zygomycetes” is historical terminology; Mucorales is used for the relevant mucormycosis group.
  • Taenia solium causes neurocysticercosis, not an ordinary pyogenic abscess. Tissue infection follows human-carrier egg ingestion; pork cysticerci cause intestinal taeniasis. Echinococcus hydatid cysts and cerebral malaria also have mechanisms distinct from bacterial pus collections.
  • Prions cause protein-misfolding neurodegeneration rather than a conventional inflammatory viral infection. The final list includes several distinct organism classes, not one interchangeable “septic” category.
  • Related applications add classic organism-identification clues, urgent diagnostic principles, and clinical host/exposure settings to the lecture lists. These include necrotizing otitis externa, Group B streptococcal CAMP positivity and E. coli K1 neonatal clues, mold/yeast morphology, malaria sequestration, and rabies prophylaxis. They are educational extensions, not claims that all those details appear explicitly on the slides.

CNS Infections

Original slide 16

Mapped concepts and practice
  • Q045 — The dominant features reflect meningeal irritation. Altered consciousness can also occur in severe meningitis, so syndromes overlap rather than remaining absolutely separate.
  • Q046 — Parenchymal dysfunction is central to encephalitis. Sustained altered mental status plus supportive findings distinguishes it from headache/neck stiffness alone.
  • Q047 — Meningeal and brain involvement produce overlapping symptoms. The term does not specify a single organism or exclude noninfectious inflammation.
  • Q048 — Postinfectious immune-mediated demyelination supports ADEM. It is an important example of postinfectious disease, but not a synonym for every postinfectious encephalopathy.
  • Q049 — AFM is a clinical/radiologic syndrome with motor gray-matter injury and nonpolio causes, including some enteroviruses. Sensory findings are often less prominent than weakness.
  • Q050 — Motor, sensory, and autonomic involvement supports transverse myelitis. Symmetry is common but not absolute, and early reflexes may be reduced during spinal shock.
