Overview of Nerve Fibers

  • Nerve fibers are neuronal axons; afferents carry receptor information toward the CNS and efferents carry commands from the CNS to effectors.
  • Somatic motor effectors are skeletal muscle; autonomic effectors include smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands. Both sensory and motor axons vary in diameter and conduction speed.
  • Conduction properties describe how rapidly information travels, whereas afferent/efferent describes its direction. A mixed peripheral nerve contains multiple fiber types.

Nerve Fiber Classification, Cont.

  • The Erlanger-Gasser scheme includes myelinated A fibers (alpha, beta, gamma, delta), small myelinated B fibers, and small unmyelinated C fibers.
  • A fibers include somatic motor and sensory axons; B fibers are classically preganglionic autonomic axons; C fibers include slow-pain afferents and postganglionic autonomic axons.
  • Do not equate a letter category with one exclusive function. The sensory-only numerical scheme I-IV overlaps with the A/C scheme but does not classify motor B fibers.

Neural Signaling · Slide 7/58

A Fibers

  • A-alpha: largest and fastest myelinated fibers; alpha motor neurons to extrafusal skeletal muscle and group I proprioceptive afferents. The slide gives approximately 80-120 m/s.
  • A-beta: large myelinated sensory fibers carrying discriminative touch, pressure, vibration, and group II proprioceptive input.
  • A-gamma: myelinated motor fibers to intrafusal muscle spindle fibers, adjusting spindle sensitivity during muscle contraction.
  • A-delta: smaller myelinated sensory fibers, especially fast sharp pain and cold; the slide also lists touch and pressure. Approximate velocity is 5-30 m/s.
  • Fiber diameters and speeds overlap and vary with temperature and measurement conditions. Recognize relative order and function rather than treating ranges as diagnostic cutoffs.

C Fibers

  • C fibers are small unmyelinated axons with continuous, slow conduction, approximately 0.5-2 m/s in the slide.
  • Somatic/visceral C afferents convey slow burning or aching pain, warmth, and other visceral signals; many postganglionic autonomic axons are also C fibers.

Neural Signaling · Slide 10/58

Conduction Velocity and Myelination

  • Myelin increases effective membrane resistance and reduces capacitance across internodes, limiting current leak and reducing the charge required to change membrane voltage.
  • Local current spreads rapidly beneath myelin; action potentials regenerate at nodes of Ranvier. This is saltatory conduction, not literal movement of a single spike through empty space.
  • Unmyelinated axons regenerate action potentials along successive membrane segments, producing slower continuous conduction.

Conduction Velocity and Myelination,

  • Nodes of Ranvier are short gaps between myelin internodes and have a high density of voltage-gated sodium channels; the figure labels dendrites, soma/nucleus, axon hillock, axon, myelin, nodes, and axon terminals.
  • The slide illustrates internodal distances around 1-2 mm and nodal lengths around 2-3 micrometers; actual dimensions vary with axon and species.
  • Internodal membrane has far fewer exposed sodium channels than nodes. 'Virtually no channels' is a simplified teaching comparison, not absence of all membrane proteins.
  • Saltatory regeneration preserves a full-sized propagated action potential; the graded local current between nodes is passive.

Clinical Relevance

  • Peripheral neuropathy can impair sensory, motor, and autonomic function depending on which peripheral axons are injured; demyelinating and axonal mechanisms differ.
  • Important correction: multiple sclerosis is a CNS demyelinating disease affecting oligodendrocyte myelin. It should not be described as a peripheral A/B-fiber disorder merely because those categories are myelinated.
  • Diabetic neuropathy can involve small A-delta/C fibers, large myelinated sensory fibers, motor fibers, and autonomic fibers. Burning pain can indicate small-fiber disease; impaired vibration and proprioception indicate large-fiber involvement.
  • Loss of protective sensation and autonomic/vascular factors contribute to diabetic foot injury; this is broader than abnormal sensory adaptation alone.

Summary of Nerve Fiber Types

  • Large myelinated A-alpha fibers conduct fastest; small unmyelinated C fibers conduct slowest. Diameter, myelin, temperature, and injury all influence speed.
  • Fiber class supports a functional inference but is not an absolute one-to-one code: a peripheral nerve contains mixtures of sensory, motor, and autonomic axons.
  • Recall the high-yield pairs: Ia spindle, Ib tendon organ, II secondary spindle/touch, III fast pain, IV slow pain.

Types of Sensory Receptors

  • Sensory receptors convert an adequate stimulus into an electrical response; modality refers to the stimulus type the receptor is best suited to detect.
  • Mechanoreceptors detect deformation: touch, pressure, vibration, stretch, hearing, and balance. Examples include Pacinian/Meissner corpuscles and cochlear hair cells.
  • Thermoreceptors detect temperature; nociceptors detect potentially damaging mechanical, thermal, or chemical stimuli; photoreceptors detect light.
  • Chemoreceptors include taste, smell, and carotid body cells. Proprioceptors detect body position, muscle length, and tension and are functionally specialized mechanoreceptors.
  • The 'electromagnetic' category overlaps with photoreceptors; these categories are not all mutually exclusive.

Neural Signaling · Slide 19/58

Mechanoreceptors: Sensing Touch and Pressure

  • Mechanical deformation gates channels and generates a graded receptor potential. If the receptor is the afferent neuron's ending, sufficient depolarization triggers action potentials at its excitable trigger region.
  • Pacinian corpuscles lie deep in dermis/subcutaneous tissue and detect high-frequency vibration and changes in deep pressure; Meissner corpuscles in glabrous skin detect light touch/flutter and slip.
  • Carotid sinus baroreceptors sense stretch associated with blood pressure. Hair cells in organ of Corti detect sound, and vestibular hair cells detect head movement/orientation.
  • Clarification: not every mechanoreceptor itself fires action potentials. Hair cells use graded potentials to regulate transmitter release onto afferent neurons.

Thermoreceptors: Detecting Temperature

  • Cutaneous warm/cold receptors change firing with local temperature; temperature-sensitive TRP cation channels participate in transduction.
  • TRPV1 is activated by noxious heat and capsaicin; TRPM8 by cooling and menthol. These channels generate a graded depolarization before afferent spikes.
  • Extreme temperatures recruit nociceptive pathways, so temperature sensation and thermal pain overlap without being identical modalities.

Nociceptors: Pain Receptors

  • Nociceptors are high-threshold sensory endings activated by potentially damaging pressure, temperature, or chemicals. Their afferent action potentials enter spinal/brainstem pain-processing circuits.
  • Fast sharp first pain is associated with A-delta axons; slower burning/aching second pain with C axons.
  • Nociception is neural detection/processing of noxious input; pain is a perceptual experience. Persistent pain can involve peripheral sensitization, central sensitization, or nerve injury, not only continued activation of intact nociceptors.

Photoreceptors: Light Detection and Vision

  • Rods and cones reside in retina: rods are highly sensitive in dim light, while cones support color and high-acuity vision in brighter light.
  • Photons alter visual pigment and initiate a cascade that closes cGMP-gated cation channels, hyperpolarizing the photoreceptor.
  • Photoreceptors alter glutamate release through graded potentials; retinal ganglion cells generate the action potentials carried by the optic nerve. The slide's reference to action potentials is a downstream result, not spikes in rods/cones.

Chemoreceptors: Sensing Chemicals (Taste & Smell)

  • Gustatory cells respond to dissolved tastants; olfactory receptor neurons detect odorants in olfactory mucosa. Chemical detection can use ion channels or GPCR second-messenger cascades.
  • Salt/sour involve ion-dependent mechanisms; sweet/bitter/umami chiefly use GPCR pathways. Olfactory receptors chiefly use a cAMP-mediated depolarizing cascade.
  • A taste receptor cell usually communicates with a distinct afferent neuron; an olfactory receptor cell is itself a primary sensory neuron.

Neural Signaling · Slide 25/58

Disorders Involving Visceral Sensory Neurons

  • Visceral sensory signals support homeostatic reflexes and convey organ distension, chemistry, and pain. Their dysfunction can contribute to visceral pain syndromes.
  • IBS can involve visceral hypersensitivity and altered gut-brain processing; it is multifactorial rather than simply destruction of visceral sensory neurons.
  • Autonomic dysreflexia usually occurs after a high spinal cord lesion, especially at/above T6: a noxious visceral stimulus below the lesion can provoke excessive sympathetic output and dangerous hypertension.
  • A full bladder or blocked urinary catheter is a classic trigger. Reflex bradycardia and sweating/flushing above the lesion can accompany vasoconstriction below it.

Board-review addition

Resting potential, action potentials, and conduction block

  • Resting membrane potential depends strongly on K+ permeability and transmembrane ion gradients. The Na+/K+-ATPase maintains gradients over time by exporting 3 Na+ and importing 2 K+ per ATP; it is not the main immediate repolarizing current of each spike.
  • Opening voltage-gated Na+ channels drives the rapid upstroke; Na+ channel inactivation plus delayed voltage-gated K+ opening drives repolarization. Continued K+ conductance can produce an afterhyperpolarization.
  • Absolute refractory period chiefly reflects Na+ channel inactivation; a larger stimulus cannot produce another normal spike until sufficient channels recover. The relative refractory period permits a spike with stronger stimulation.
  • Spike amplitude is approximately all-or-none for a given axon and condition; stimulus intensity is chiefly encoded by firing rate and recruited fibers, not larger spikes. Receptor/synaptic potentials can summate; action potentials do not add their amplitudes.
  • Myelin raises membrane resistance and lowers capacitance; larger diameter lowers axial resistance. Demyelination slows conduction and can cause conduction block by reducing the safety factor for exciting the next region.
  • Local anesthetics block voltage-gated Na+ channels and interrupt action-potential propagation. Frequency/use dependence favors activated channels; fiber susceptibility also depends on diameter, myelin, position, and drug exposure rather than a universal size-only rule.
  • At a chemical synapse, a terminal spike opens voltage-gated Ca2+ channels; Ca2+ drives vesicle fusion and transmitter release. Postsynaptic receptor conductance changes can cause excitation, inhibition, or shunting depending on reversal potential.
  • EPSPs/IPSPs are graded and summate across space/time. The axon initial segment integrates whether combined input reaches threshold; a depolarizing conductance can still be inhibitory if it holds voltage below threshold and shunts other input.

Receptor comparison, receptive fields, and coding

  • Pacinian: deep dermis/subcutis, large receptive field, rapid adaptation, high-frequency vibration. Meissner: superficial dermal papillae in glabrous skin, small field, rapid adaptation, flutter/slip.
  • Merkel: epidermal touch complexes, small field, slow adaptation, edges/form and sustained pressure. Ruffini: deeper endings, larger field, slow adaptation, skin stretch.
  • Small receptive fields, dense innervation, and lateral inhibition support fine spatial discrimination. Two-point discrimination is better at fingertips than back because of peripheral and central organization.
  • A labeled line identifies modality through the activated neural pathway; frequency and population recruitment encode stimulus strength, while receptive-field patterns encode location.
  • Primary sensory endings, such as Pacinian afferents, generate a local generator potential in the same neuron that later spikes. Separate receptor cells, such as hair cells and photoreceptors, regulate transmitter release onto downstream neurons.
  • Adequate stimulus means the energy type to which a receptor is most sensitive; electrical activation of a sensory pathway can still create the modality normally carried by that pathway.
  • Adaptation emphasizes change while tonic responses retain information. A spike-frequency plot with onset/offset firing suggests a phasic receptor; sustained firing with a smaller decline suggests a tonic receptor.

Board-review addition

Retinal, hair-cell, taste, and smell mechanisms

  • Rod in dark: relatively high cGMP → open outer-segment cation channels → inward dark current and relative depolarization → tonic glutamate release. Light reverses this through rhodopsin/transducin/PDE and lowers glutamate.
  • The retinal chromophore is derived from vitamin A; deficiency can impair dim-light vision. Rods favor sensitivity, while foveal cones favor acuity/color.
  • ON bipolar cells use a sign-inverting glutamate response: less photoreceptor glutamate in light permits depolarization. OFF bipolar circuits generally respond with the opposite sign. Do not infer that every downstream retinal neuron hyperpolarizes because the rod does.
  • Cochlear hair-cell stereocilia deflection toward the tallest row opens tip-link-associated channels; K+ enters from endolymph, depolarizing the cell. Basolateral Ca2+ channels then support glutamate release onto auditory afferents.
  • Olfaction uses odorant GPCR/Golf → adenylyl cyclase → cAMP → cation channel opening. Phototransduction uses transducin → PDE → decreased cGMP → cation channel closure.
  • Sweet, bitter, and umami use GPCR pathways, including PLC/IP3 and intracellular Ca2+ signaling. Salt can use direct cation entry; sour uses proton-dependent mechanisms with a central role for OTOP1.
  • Taste afferents travel through VII from much of anterior tongue, IX from posterior tongue, and X from epiglottic regions to the solitary nucleus. The old tongue map assigning one taste exclusively to each region is incorrect.
  • Photoreceptor light adaptation, mechanical end-organ adaptation, and central habituation are distinct mechanisms; use the pathway to predict the sign and timing of response rather than assigning all receptors one response pattern.

Board-review addition

Pain, small-fiber neuropathy, and sensitization

  • A-delta transmits relatively fast localized first pain; C fibers transmit slower diffuse burning/aching second pain. Small-fiber injury can impair pain/temperature despite preserved large-fiber vibration and normal routine nerve-conduction studies.
  • Nociceptors generally adapt little or slowly, and inflammation can lower their thresholds. Hyperalgesia is exaggerated pain to a painful stimulus; allodynia is pain to an innocuous stimulus.
  • Peripheral sensitization can involve inflammatory mediators; central sensitization includes increased excitability and altered inhibition in spinal/brain circuits. Neuropathic pain can arise without continuing external tissue injury.
  • Dorsal horn inhibitory circuits can modulate nociceptive transmission; rubbing an injured area can recruit large-fiber touch input that reduces pain signaling in a gate-control model.
  • Descending pathways from brainstem can modulate spinal pain transmission using monoaminergic and endogenous opioid mechanisms. Pain intensity is not simply the number of activated receptors.
  • Tinnitus, anosmia, IBS, and sensory features of autism have multiple possible mechanisms. An adaptation concept can help explain some physiology without serving as a complete diagnosis or causal account.

Board-review addition

Spinal tract map and crossing rules

  • Dorsal-column medial-lemniscal pathway: first-order sensory soma in DRG → ipsilateral dorsal column → gracile/cuneate nucleus in caudal medulla → internal arcuate crossing → medial lemniscus → VPL thalamus → somatosensory cortex.
  • Fasciculus gracilis lies medially and carries lower-body input; fasciculus cuneatus lies laterally above roughly T6 and carries upper-body input. Vibration, conscious position sense, and discriminative touch are key modalities.
  • Spinothalamic pathway: DRG → short ascending/descending branches in Lissauer tract → dorsal horn synapse → crossing in anterior white commissure over nearby segments → contralateral anterolateral ascent → thalamus/cortex.
  • Lateral corticospinal tract: cortex → internal capsule → cerebral peduncle → pons → medullary pyramid → pyramidal decussation → contralateral lateral cord. In the spinal cord it controls ipsilateral muscles below the lesion.
  • Anterior horn/root injury gives LMN signs at that segment; descending tract interruption gives UMN signs below after spinal shock resolves. One cord injury can therefore produce both patterns at different levels.
  • Spinocerebellar pathways carry largely unconscious proprioceptive information. Dorsal spinocerebellar input ascends mainly ipsilaterally through the inferior cerebellar peduncle; ventral pathways have crossing/recrossing patterns resulting in functional ipsilateral representation.
  • The original repeated cord-section plate labels gracile/cuneate, posterior/anterior spinocerebellar, anterolateral, lateral/anterior corticospinal, lateral/medial reticulospinal, and lateral vestibulospinal pathways. These support sensory transmission, coordinated movement, and axial/postural control alongside the three major localization pathways.
  • A sensory level points toward a cord process; dermatomal radiating pain with reduced segmental reflexes favors a root. Clinical patterns can be incomplete and overlapping.

Board-review addition

Cord blood supply and acute versus chronic spinal injury

  • One anterior spinal artery supplies much of anterior two-thirds of cord; paired posterior spinal arteries preferentially supply posterior regions. Segmental radiculomedullary feeders reinforce these longitudinal vessels.
  • Artery of Adamkiewicz is especially important to lower-cord anterior supply. Its origin is variable, commonly left lower thoracic/upper lumbar; operative injury can cause anterior-cord ischemia.
  • Spinal shock is loss of reflexes/tone after acute severe cord injury. Neurogenic shock is hypotension due to sympathetic loss, often with bradycardia in high-cord injury. Hemorrhagic shock remains an important alternative in trauma and commonly causes compensatory tachycardia.
  • Autonomic dysreflexia is episodic severe hypertension from an unmodulated sympathetic reflex, usually with a lesion at/above T6. Bladder distension, catheter obstruction, or bowel impaction are classic triggers.
  • Dysreflexia can produce pounding headache, sweating/flushing above the lesion, and reflex bradycardia. Sit the patient upright and address the trigger promptly while monitoring and treating dangerous pressure as clinically indicated.
  • Central cord syndrome after cervical hyperextension classically causes greater arm than leg weakness; this is distinct from a small central syrinx causing segmental crossing-fiber sensory loss. The arm-predominant pattern should not be reduced to a rigid universally proven corticospinal lamination model.
  • Complete high cervical injury can impair diaphragmatic function via C3-C5; respiratory assessment is central to acute localization.

Board-review addition

Metabolic, infectious, and inherited tract diseases

  • B12 deficiency can follow pernicious anemia/intrinsic-factor loss, gastric or ileal disease, inadequate intake, or other causes. Methylmalonic acid and homocysteine can rise; folate deficiency raises homocysteine without the characteristic methylmalonic acid increase.
  • Nitrous oxide can inactivate B12 and cause a myeloneuropathy even when a measured B12 concentration is not clearly low. Copper deficiency can mimic a posterior-column/corticospinal syndrome.
  • Treating folate deficiency alone can improve anemia while failing to address unrecognized B12-related neurologic injury. Neurologic symptoms may occur without macrocytosis.
  • Tabes dorsalis injures dorsal roots and columns: sensory ataxia, areflexia, lightning pains, and possible light-near dissociation. B12 SCD adds corticospinal signs; a clinical combination guides the differential.
  • Friedreich ataxia: usually biallelic FXN GAA expansions; mitochondrial dysfunction, early progressive ataxia, sensory loss, areflexia with possible Babinski, cardiomyopathy, and diabetes. Frataxin disease is autosomal recessive rather than a typical dominant CAG disorder.
  • SMN1-related SMA: anterior horn degeneration and preserved sensation; additional SMN2 copies often mitigate severity. Polio is an infectious anterior horn disease rather than an inherited repeat disorder.
  • ALS classically combines UMN and LMN deficits with relative sensory sparing. Bulbar and respiratory involvement matter; frontotemporal features occur in a subset.
  • Progressive weakness with substantial sensory loss, a clear sensory level, or prominent early sphincter dysfunction warrants consideration of ALS mimics such as myelopathy rather than assuming ALS from fasciculations alone.

A practical syndrome comparison and emergency localization

  • Hemicord/Brown-Séquard: ipsilateral weakness and position/vibration loss below; contralateral pain-temperature loss starting below; possible ipsilateral segmental LMN and sensory deficits at injury.
  • Anterior cord: bilateral weakness plus pain-temperature loss with relatively spared posterior-column modalities. Posterior cord: position/vibration loss with relative motor and pain-temperature sparing.
  • Central cervical syrinx: bilateral suspended pain-temperature loss, then hand LMN weakness if anterior horns are reached; later corticospinal/sympathetic findings with expansion.
  • Sensory ataxia improves with visual guidance and worsens on eye closure; cerebellar ataxia persists with eyes open and can have dysmetria/intention tremor. Vestibular dysfunction often adds vertigo/nystagmus.
  • Conus medullaris involves terminal cord and may have mixed signs, symmetric saddle loss, and early sphincter dysfunction; cauda equina affects roots and tends toward asymmetric painful LMN deficits. Neither pattern is perfectly exclusive.
  • New urinary retention or altered bladder sensation plus saddle anesthesia or progressive leg deficits suggests compressive conus/cauda disease and requires urgent MRI/evaluation, not routine follow-up.
  • A complete cord lesion can initially be flaccid/areflexic; later spasticity does not mean the lesion moved from peripheral nerve to cord. Timing is part of localization.

Board-review addition

Reading every spinal cross-section landmark

  • Posterior median sulcus and septum mark the dorsal midline; the deeper anterior median fissure marks the ventral midline. Use these landmarks to orient a section before assigning lesion laterality.
  • Posterior, lateral, and anterior funiculi/white columns contain ascending and descending axons. Dorsal horns receive sensory input; ventral horns contain somatic motor neurons; thoracolumbar lateral/intermediate gray includes sympathetic preganglionic neurons.
  • Posterior root is afferent; anterior root is efferent. A spinal nerve and its dorsal/ventral rami are mixed, so a root and a ramus are not interchangeable.
  • Rexed laminae divide spinal gray matter into zones I-X. Lamina II/substantia gelatinosa is important in pain modulation; lamina IX contains somatic motor neuron groups; lamina X surrounds the central canal. The Roman numerals in the original plate indicate these laminae.
  • The anterior white commissure lies ventral to the central canal and carries crossing pain-temperature axons; this is the key early syrinx target. A central cavity can later expand into nearby motor and long-tract structures.
  • In dorsal columns, sacral/lumbar input lies more medially and upper thoracic/cervical input is added laterally. The S/L/T/C labels in the figure indicate somatotopy, not separate spinal nerves.
  • Denticulate ligaments are lateral pial extensions that help stabilize the cord within its dural covering. They are supporting structures rather than white-matter tracts.
  • Gracile/cuneate pathways convey conscious discriminative sensation; spinocerebellar pathways support unconscious proprioceptive coordination; corticospinal conveys descending motor control; vestibulo- and reticulospinal pathways contribute posture and automatic motor adjustments.
