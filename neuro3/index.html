Brainstem Organization

  • Brainstem consists of midbrain, pons, and medulla; it links cerebrum, cerebellum, and spinal cord and contains cranial motor/sensory nuclei, long tracts, and autonomic networks.
  • Tectum is the dorsal midbrain roof behind the aqueduct; tegmentum is the central region; basis is the ventral region containing major descending fibers and, in pons, pontine nuclei.
  • Orient a section in sequence: level → dorsal/ventral → medial/lateral. A ventral peduncle/pyramid is not interchangeable with a dorsal sensory nucleus.

External Brainstem Anatomy

  • Midbrain landmarks: ventral cerebral peduncles and dorsal superior/inferior colliculi. Superior colliculi participate in visual orienting; inferior colliculi in auditory processing/orienting.

Cranial Nerve Attachment Sites

  • CN III attaches ventrally to midbrain near the interpeduncular region; CN IV is unique in exiting dorsally below the inferior colliculus before wrapping around the brainstem.
  • CN V attaches to lateral pons. CN VI attaches near the midline pontomedullary junction; VII and VIII attach more laterally near the cerebellopontine angle.
  • CN IX and X emerge lateral to the medullary olive; XII emerges between pyramid and olive. Attachment location helps infer the level of a focal lesion.
  • Spinal CN XI rootlets arise from upper cervical cord, ascend through foramen magnum, then leave through jugular foramen. They are not simply another nucleus confined to medulla.

Blood Supply – Circle of Willis

  • Internal carotid circulation gives ACA and MCA. Paired vertebral arteries supply PICA branches and unite to form basilar artery.
  • Basilar branches include pontine arteries, AICA, and SCA, then it terminates in paired PCAs. Variants are common, so these are the usual anatomical relationships.
  • Anterior communicating artery connects the ACAs; posterior communicating arteries link internal carotid circulation with PCAs.
  • The circle proper includes proximal ACA/PCA segments, communicating arteries, and terminal internal carotid connections; MCA and cerebellar arteries are adjacent branches rather than components of the ring.

Major Arterial Supply

  • Midbrain is supplied by branches from PCA and SCA, with contributions varying by level and precise territory.
  • Pons is supplied by basilar paramedian/circumferential branches and contributions from AICA and SCA; medial and lateral territories differ.
  • Medulla is supplied by vertebral, PICA, and anterior spinal branches. Medial medulla classically involves ASA/paramedian vertebral supply; lateral medulla commonly involves vertebral or PICA territory.
  • An artery name alone does not specify every structure injured; combine vascular territory with the clinical and cross-sectional pattern.

Cranial Nerve Functional Components

  • GSE/general somatic efferent supplies somite-related skeletal muscles: ocular motor III/IV/VI and tongue XII are major examples.
  • SVE/special visceral efferent is branchial motor to pharyngeal-arch-derived skeletal muscle, including mastication V3, facial expression VII, and stylopharyngeus/pharyngeal-laryngeal muscles IX/X. 'Visceral' here does not mean smooth muscle.
  • GVE/general visceral efferent is autonomic, here mainly preganglionic parasympathetic output in III, VII, IX, and X to glands, smooth muscle, and heart.
  • SVA/special visceral afferent carries smell and taste; GVA/general visceral afferent carries internal-organ and reflex sensory input; GSA/general somatic afferent carries general touch, pain, temperature, and related somatic sensation.
  • SSA/special somatic afferent carries vision, hearing, and balance. Efferent means away from CNS; afferent means toward CNS.

Organization of Cranial Nerve Nuclei

  • Opening of the embryonic hindbrain into fourth ventricle spreads sensory alar-plate derivatives laterally; motor basal-plate derivatives remain more medial.
  • Sulcus limitans separates motor/efferent and sensory/afferent territories. The functional-column diagram is a developmental framework, not a rigid straight line through every adult nucleus.
  • GSE, branchial motor/SVE, and autonomic/GVE nuclei occupy motor territory; visceral/taste, somatic sensory, and vestibulocochlear nuclei occupy sensory territory.
  • Solitary nucleus receives both SVA taste, mainly rostrally, and GVA visceral input, mainly caudally. A shared nucleus does not make taste a GSA modality.

  • Typical level map: III/IV → midbrain; V/VI/VII and much of VIII → pons/pontomedullary region; IX/X/XII → medulla; spinal XI → upper cervical cord.

Anatomical Actions vs Clinical Testing Watch the video:

  • Anatomical action depends on the muscle's line of pull and the current eye position. Clinical testing positions the globe to preferentially isolate a vertical component.
  • Medial rectus adducts; lateral rectus abducts. Superior/inferior recti elevate/depress most selectively when the eye is abducted; obliques are tested for vertical movement with the eye adducted.
  • Superior rectus also intorts/adducts; inferior rectus also extorts/adducts; superior oblique also intorts/abducts; inferior oblique also extorts/abducts.
  • The phrase 'down and in' describes testing superior oblique with an adducted eye, not its full anatomical vector or a claim that superior oblique itself primarily adducts.

Extraocular Muscles: Actions & Testing

  • Clinical H-pattern: medial rectus → adduct; lateral rectus → abduct; superior rectus → abduct then elevate; inferior rectus → abduct then depress.

Oculomotor Nerve (CN III)

  • Oculomotor GSE neurons in midbrain supply most extraocular muscles and levator; Edinger-Westphal preganglionic GVE neurons provide ocular parasympathetic output.
  • CN III travels near the posterior communicating region, through cavernous sinus lateral wall, and enters the orbit through superior orbital fissure.
  • Parasympathetic route: Edinger-Westphal → III → ciliary ganglion synapse → short ciliary nerves → sphincter pupillae and ciliary muscle.
  • Sphincter contraction causes miosis; ciliary muscle contraction allows the lens to become rounder for near accommodation. These are separate from skeletal extraocular movement.

Oculomotor Nerve Palsy

  • A complete peripheral III palsy can cause ptosis, a down-and-out eye from relatively unopposed VI/IV actions, diplopia, impaired accommodation, and mydriasis if parasympathetic fibers are affected.
  • Compressive lesions such as a PCOM-region aneurysm often involve superficial pupilloconstrictor fibers; microvascular ischemia often relatively spares the pupil.
  • Pupil sparing is a tendency, not a reliable rule excluding an aneurysm or other compression. Acute painful/new III palsy requires appropriate urgent assessment rather than reassurance from the pupil alone.
  • The 3Ds aid recall of a typical complete palsy but incomplete lesions may lack one or more findings.

Trochlear Nerve (CN IV)

  • Trochlear nucleus lies in caudal midbrain near inferior colliculus; axons decussate in the superior medullary velum and exit dorsally.
  • CN IV travels through cavernous sinus lateral wall and superior orbital fissure to superior oblique. Its long intracranial course makes it vulnerable to trauma.
  • Superior oblique intorts the eye and depresses an adducted eye; testing 'down and in' isolates its depressive action.
  • A pre-crossing trochlear nuclear lesion weakens the contralateral superior oblique; a peripheral IV nerve lesion weakens the ipsilateral muscle.

Trochlear Nerve Palsy

  • IV palsy produces vertical/torsional diplopia, especially when looking downward with the eye adducted, such as reading or descending stairs.
  • The affected eye can be relatively elevated and extorted. Patients often tilt the head away from the affected side to reduce misalignment.
  • Do not confuse head tilt away in IV palsy with head turn toward the affected side often used in VI palsy; compensatory postures depend on the alignment problem.

Abducens Nerve (CN VI)

  • Abducens nucleus lies in caudal pons; the nerve emerges near midline at the pontomedullary junction and innervates ipsilateral lateral rectus.
  • Its intracranial route includes a long course near the clivus/Dorello canal and cavernous sinus adjacent to the internal carotid before entering superior orbital fissure.
  • Peripheral VI output abducts one eye. The abducens nucleus also contains interneurons coordinating opposite medial rectus through the MLF, so a nuclear lesion can impair conjugate gaze rather than only one muscle.

Abducens Nerve Palsy

  • Peripheral VI palsy weakens lateral rectus: the affected eye cannot abduct fully and may rest medially in esotropia.
  • Horizontal diplopia worsens looking toward the affected side, and patients may turn the head toward that side to reduce required eye excursion.
  • Raised intracranial pressure can cause a VI palsy as a false-localizing sign; an isolated VI deficit does not always identify a pontine infarct.

Trigeminal Nerve (CN V)

  • CN V attaches to pons and is the major somatic sensory nerve of face, cornea, oral/nasal regions, teeth, and much dura.
  • V1 and V2 are sensory; V3 contains somatic sensory and branchial motor components. Trigeminal ganglion contains most primary sensory somata; motor fibers bypass its sensory relay role.
  • V1 broadly supplies forehead/cornea/upper nose; V2 midface/upper teeth; V3 lower face/lower teeth and anterior tongue general sensation, with motor fibers to mastication.
  • The mandibular angle is an important regional exception, receiving upper cervical rather than typical trigeminal cutaneous supply.

Trigeminal Divisions & Skull Foramina

  • V1 exits through superior orbital fissure, V2 through foramen rotundum, and V3 through foramen ovale.

Central vs Peripheral Facial Weakness

  • Most cranial motor nuclei receive bilateral corticobulbar input; lower facial motor pools and genioglossus receive predominantly contralateral drive.
  • A unilateral supranuclear/UMN lesion classically weakens contralateral lower face with relative forehead sparing because upper-face input is more bilateral.
  • A facial nucleus/fascicle or peripheral VII lesion produces ipsilateral upper and lower facial weakness, including brow elevation and eye closure. An LMN pattern can therefore occur from a pontine lesion, not only a peripheral nerve lesion.
  • Bell palsy is an idiopathic peripheral VII palsy, a diagnosis requiring an appropriate clinical pattern; forehead involvement alone does not identify its cause.
  • Forehead sparing is a useful tendency rather than an absolute rule in every central facial lesion.

Long Tracts in the Brainstem

  • Corticospinal fibers cross at pyramidal decussation in caudal medulla; a typical brainstem lesion above that crossing causes contralateral limb weakness.
  • Dorsal-column sensory axons relay in gracile/cuneate nuclei, then internal arcuate fibers cross in caudal medulla to form medial lemniscus. Above crossing, injury produces contralateral vibration/position loss.

  • At a decussation or in a complex lesion, findings may not fit a simple single-side rule; the standard patterns assume one tract segment on one side.

Board-review addition

All twelve cranial nerves, foramina, and functional patterns

  • CN I: olfactory receptor axons traverse cribriform plate to olfactory bulb; anterior cranial base trauma can cause anosmia and CSF rhinorrhea. CN II: retinal ganglion axons traverse optic canal; lesions affect vision and afferent pupillary signaling.
  • CN III/IV/VI use superior orbital fissure; V1 also uses this fissure, V2 rotundum, V3 ovale. VII/VIII enter internal acoustic meatus; VII later exits stylomastoid foramen. IX/X/XI leave jugular foramen; XII uses hypoglossal canal. Spinal XI first enters through foramen magnum.
  • Predominantly sensory: I, II, VIII; predominantly somatic/branchial motor: III, IV, VI, XI, XII; mixed: V, VII, IX, X. III also carries parasympathetics despite a simplified 'motor' classification.
  • Parasympathetic ganglia: III → ciliary; VII → pterygopalatine and submandibular; IX → otic; X → near/intramural ganglia of thoracoabdominal organs.
  • Trigeminal branches often distribute autonomic fibers originating from other cranial nerves. A fiber traveling on V does not necessarily originate in a trigeminal parasympathetic nucleus.
  • GSE refers to motor output to somite-related skeletal muscle; SVE is branchial motor to skeletal muscle derived from pharyngeal arches; GVE is autonomic. SVA = smell/taste; SSA = vision/hearing/balance; GSA = general somatic; GVA = visceral sensory.
  • Skull-base combinations help localization: jugular foramen disease can affect IX/X/XI; an extended skull-base lesion involving XII adds tongue weakness. Internal acoustic meatus/CPA lesions can combine VIII with VII and, if larger, V findings.
  • Visual localization: one optic nerve lesion causes ipsilateral monocular loss; midline chiasmal compression can cause bitemporal loss by injuring crossing nasal retinal fibers; retrochiasmal lesions cause contralateral homonymous field deficits, with details depending on location.

Board-review addition

Pupillary reflexes, gaze circuits, and ocular palsy differentiation

  • Light reflex: retina/II → pretectal region → bilateral Edinger-Westphal nuclei → III → ciliary ganglia → short ciliary nerves → sphincter pupillae. Bilateral central projection explains direct and consensual constriction.
  • A complete left II afferent lesion prevents either pupil from responding to light shone in the left eye; both can respond when the intact right eye is illuminated if III efferents are intact.
  • A left III parasympathetic efferent lesion prevents left pupil constriction regardless of which eye is illuminated; the right pupil can still constrict to either light input if afferents are intact.
  • Near response includes convergence through medial recti, accommodation through ciliary muscle, and miosis. It overlaps with light-reflex efferents but uses different upstream circuitry.
  • Horizontal gaze: PPRF activates ipsilateral VI nucleus; lateral rectus abducts that eye, while interneurons cross and ascend in opposite MLF to III medial rectus neurons.
  • An isolated VI nerve lesion impairs ipsilateral abduction; a VI nucleus/PPRF lesion can impair both eyes looking toward the lesion. A facial colliculus lesion may also affect looping VII fibers.
  • MLF injury causes internuclear ophthalmoplegia: ipsilateral adduction weakness on horizontal gaze with nystagmus of the abducting eye. Convergence can be preserved, though not universally. Bilateral INO suggests demyelination in the appropriate young-adult setting; vascular lesions are important in older adults.
  • Myasthenia can mimic ocular palsy with fluctuating ptosis/diplopia, typically sparing the pupils. Horner syndrome gives miosis and mild ptosis from sympathetic interruption, contrasting with a complete compressive III pattern.
  • Acute III palsy, especially with pain or pupillary involvement, raises concern for aneurysm/compression. Pupil sparing alone does not safely exclude a serious cause.

Board-review addition

Trigeminal and facial lesion localization

  • V3 mastication muscles include masseter/temporalis/pterygoids plus mylohyoid, anterior digastric, tensor tympani, and tensor veli palatini. VII supplies buccinator, facial expression, stapedius, posterior digastric, and stylohyoid.
  • Tensor tympani is V3; stapedius is VII. Hyperacusis after VII injury reflects failure to damp stapes motion. Buccal VII is motor to facial muscles; long buccal V3 is sensory to cheek mucosa/skin.
  • VII lesion proximal to greater petrosal can reduce tearing plus taste/saliva/stapedius/movement; a lesion distal to greater petrosal may spare tears; distal to stylomastoid usually preserves intracanalicular taste/autonomic branches.
  • Chorda tympani crosses middle ear and joins lingual V3. A distal lingual injury after that union can impair both anterior-tongue general sensation and taste, plus related salivary fibers.
  • Bell palsy is idiopathic peripheral VII weakness. Facial weakness with ear pain/vesicles suggests Ramsay Hunt syndrome; a pontine lesion can also produce an LMN facial pattern with other brainstem findings.
  • A parotid operation can injure VII terminal motor branches without directly abolishing IX-derived parotid secretion. Geniculate is a sensory ganglion; pterygopalatine/submandibular are parasympathetic relay ganglia.
  • Trigeminal neuralgia classically causes brief electric-shock facial pain, often V2/V3 and triggered by light contact or chewing; substantial persistent sensory loss should prompt assessment for additional structural pathology.
  • Corneal reflex distinguishes input from output: a V1 lesion removes bilateral response to stimulation of that cornea; a VII lesion removes one eyelid's closure to stimulation from either side.

Board-review addition

Auditory and vestibular bedside distinctions

  • Conductive hearing loss impairs transmission through external/middle ear; sensorineural loss involves cochlea or neural pathways. CN VIII lesions cause the latter rather than a simple mechanical air-conduction block.
  • Rinne: air conduction normally exceeds bone conduction and usually remains so in sensorineural loss. Bone conduction greater than air suggests conductive loss in that ear; profound unilateral sensorineural loss can produce a false-negative Rinne through crossover.
  • Weber lateralizes toward conductive loss and away from unilateral sensorineural loss. Interpret together with Rinne and audiometry rather than treating one bedside test as definitive.
  • Vestibular schwannoma often causes progressive asymmetric hearing loss/tinnitus/imbalance; Ménière disease causes recurrent vertigo with fluctuating hearing loss/tinnitus/fullness; BPPV causes brief position-triggered attacks, usually without new hearing loss.
  • A unilateral vestibular imbalance can cause nystagmus; direction, triggers, fixation effects, other neurologic signs, and time course help differentiate peripheral from central causes.
  • Vestibulo-ocular reflex links VIII input to III/IV/VI motor output to stabilize retinal images during head motion. Cerebellar and brainstem lesions can disrupt this circuitry.
  • Bilateral vestibular schwannomas suggest NF2-related schwannomatosis; unilateral tumors are commonly sporadic. CPA extension can affect V/VII or cerebellar/brainstem structures.

Board-review addition

Bulbar, laryngeal, neck, and tongue localization

  • IX stylopharyngeus and X pharyngeal plexus/laryngeal branches coordinate swallowing; X carries motor to most palate/pharynx/larynx except V3 tensor veli palatini and IX stylopharyngeus.
  • External superior laryngeal → cricothyroid → high-pitch control. Internal superior laryngeal → sensation above folds. RLN → intrinsic muscles except cricothyroid and sensation below folds.
  • Left RLN loops under aortic arch; right under subclavian. Mediastinal pathology can affect left RLN; neck/thyroid procedures can injure either laryngeal route.
  • Proximal X weakness can cause ipsilateral palate droop, uvula away, hoarseness, and dysphagia. Isolated RLN injury can spare palate and mainly alter voice; bilateral vocal-fold abductor dysfunction may threaten breathing.
  • Right XI lesion → weak right shrug and weak leftward head turn; right XII LMN lesion → right tongue wasting/fasciculations and rightward protrusion. Test actions rather than memorizing a direction without the muscle.
  • Anterior tongue general sensation = lingual V3; taste = VII. Posterior tongue both = IX, with circumvallate taste usually IX. Epiglottic region = X. Motor = XII except palatoglossus X.
  • Bilateral corticobulbar injury can produce pseudobulbar features such as brisk jaw reflex and emotional lability; lower motor/bulbar injury more often gives wasting/fasciculations and impaired motor output. Both can threaten swallowing.

Cross-sectional landmarks and the nuclei map

  • Superior colliculus + III/EW + red nucleus → rostral midbrain. Inferior colliculus + IV + superior cerebellar peduncle decussation → caudal midbrain. Aqueduct is the midbrain CSF landmark.
  • Large basis pontis + transverse fibers/middle cerebellar peduncle → pons. V sensory/motor nuclei → middle pons. VI/VII loop/facial colliculus → caudal pons.
  • Olive + open fourth ventricle + inferior cerebellar peduncle → rostral medulla. XII/dorsal motor X/ambiguus/solitary are medullary nuclear clues. Central canal + gracile/cuneate nuclei/decussations → caudal closed medulla.
  • Cerebellar peduncles: middle mainly carries pontocerebellar afferents; superior carries major cerebellar output and crosses in caudal midbrain; inferior carries varied cerebellar afferents and some efferents, including vestibular/olivary connections.
  • MLF links ocular motor and vestibular systems; periaqueductal gray surrounds the aqueduct and participates in pain/autonomic networks; substantia nigra is a dopaminergic motor-system structure; inferior olive projects climbing-fiber information to cerebellum.
  • Functional nuclei: III/IV/VI/XII are GSE; V/VII/ambiguus are branchial motor; EW/superior and inferior salivatory/dorsal motor X are GVE; solitary is SVA/GVA; trigeminal sensory complex is GSA; vestibulocochlear is SSA.
  • The chart's accessory/ambiguus connection reflects traditional cranial-XI terminology. Spinal XI arises in cervical cord and is functionally distinct from vagal pharyngeal/laryngeal motor output.
  • Somatic and visceral mucosal-afferent labels vary among references; nerve territory, reflex role, and central target are usually more robust localization anchors than one contested letter classification.

Board-review addition

A structured cranial nerve examination and localization method

  • I: assess familiar nonirritant odors when indicated; II: visual acuity/fields, fundus, and afferent light responses; III/IV/VI: pupils, ptosis, alignment, and extraocular movements.
  • V: compare face sensory divisions and jaw strength; VII: brow elevation, tight eye closure, smile, and cheek/lip movements; VIII: hearing and vestibular complaints/examination.
  • IX/X: voice quality, swallowing, palatal elevation and selected gag testing; XI: shoulder shrug and resisted head rotation; XII: tongue protrusion, strength, wasting, and fasciculations.
  • Afferent versus efferent reflex analysis asks two questions: does stimulation on each side reach the brainstem, and can each output structure execute the response? Test the two inputs separately rather than only observing one eyelid/pupil once.
  • Ipsilateral cranial nerve signs with contralateral limb motor/sensory signs favor brainstem localization. A peripheral cranial nerve lesion alone should not produce a contralateral body long-tract deficit.
  • Cortical corticobulbar injury commonly gives contralateral lower-face and genioglossus weakness; a cranial nucleus/fascicle/nerve LMN lesion usually affects the corresponding ipsilateral muscles, with special crossing exceptions such as trochlear nuclei.
  • Separate nerve from nucleus, movement from sensation, and anatomic action from test position. Then combine level, side, modalities, and vascular territory into one explanation.
